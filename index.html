Study methods

Five methods, explained plainly.

None of these is magic, and you don't need all of them. Most students do well by combining two: one for managing time and attention, one for actually remembering things.

01

The Pomodoro Technique

For: getting started, and keeping going

Developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s and named after a tomato-shaped kitchen timer (pomodoro is Italian for tomato). You work on a single task for a fixed stretch, then take a short, real break.

How to do it

  1. Pick one task and write it down.
  2. Set a timer for 25 minutes and work only on that task.
  3. When it rings, stop and take a 5-minute break away from the screen.
  4. After four sessions, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

Why it helps: a short, fixed block makes a big task easier to begin, and regular breaks help you notice when attention is slipping. The 25 minutes is a starting point, not a rule. Adjust it to suit you.

02

Active recall

For: remembering what you've learned

Instead of rereading or highlighting, you close the book and try to bring the information back from memory. Studies of the "testing effect" have repeatedly found that practising retrieval leads to better long-term retention than reviewing the same material again.

How to do it

  1. Read or listen to a section once, carefully.
  2. Close your notes and write down, or say aloud, everything you can remember.
  3. Open your notes and check. Mark what you missed or got wrong.
  4. Turn the gaps into questions or flashcards and test yourself again later.

Why it helps: it feels harder than rereading, and that effort is the point. Rereading makes material feel familiar. Recall shows you what you actually know.

03

Spaced repetition

For: making learning last until the exam

You review material several times, with longer gaps between each review. This builds on the "spacing effect", first described by the psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus in the 1880s: we forget quickly after learning something, and each well-timed review slows that forgetting.

How to do it

  1. Learn the material and test yourself on it the same day.
  2. Review it again after about 1 day, then 3 days, then a week, then two weeks.
  3. If you get something wrong, bring it back to a shorter gap.
  4. Use a paper flashcard box (the Leitner system) or a flashcard app to keep track.

04

The Cornell note-taking method

For: notes you can actually revise from

Created by Walter Pauk, an education professor at Cornell University, in the 1950s. You divide a page into three areas, so that every page of notes becomes its own revision sheet.

Notes column
The large right-hand area. Take notes here during the lesson or while reading: short sentences, abbreviations, diagrams. Leave space between ideas.
Cue column
The narrow left-hand column. After class, write questions and key words that line up with your notes, such as "What causes X?"
Summary
The strip at the bottom. In two or three sentences, write the main idea of the page in your own words.

How to do it: the five Rs

  1. Record the lesson in the notes column.
  2. Reduce your notes to questions and key words in the cue column.
  3. Recite: cover the notes column and answer the cues out loud.
  4. Reflect on how the ideas connect, and write the summary.
  5. Review the page regularly, starting with the cues.

Why it helps: the cue column turns your notes into a built-in self-test, so Cornell notes combine naturally with active recall.

05

The Feynman technique

For: checking you truly understand something

Named after the physicist Richard Feynman, who was known for explaining difficult ideas in simple words. The steps below are a popular summary inspired by his approach, rather than a method he wrote down himself.

How to do it

  1. Write the name of a concept at the top of a blank page.
  2. Explain it in plain language, as if teaching a younger student.
  3. Wherever you get stuck or reach for jargon, go back to your textbook or notes.
  4. Simplify your explanation again, and use an example or analogy.

Why it helps: if you can't explain it simply, you've found exactly the part you need to study next. It works especially well for science, maths and any subject built on ideas rather than lists.

A distraction-free study space

Set the room up before you sit down.

You don't need a special room. A kitchen table, a library corner or your bedroom desk can all work if a few small things are in place.

Phone in another room

Not face down on the desk. Out of sight. Even a silent phone within reach pulls at your attention. If you need it for a timer, use this page instead.

One tab, one task

Close everything you don't need for the current task. Turn off notifications on your computer, or use your device's focus mode, for the length of a session.

Good light

Daylight from the side is ideal. In the evening, use a desk lamp as well as the ceiling light so you're not straining your eyes over the page.

A clear surface

Only what this session needs: the book, your notes, a pen, water. Clutter is a list of other things you could be doing.

Quiet, or steady sound

Silence suits most people. If it's noisy at home, earplugs or steady background sound, like rain or a fan, can help more than music with lyrics.

Water and real breaks

Keep water nearby. In your breaks, stand up, stretch, look out of a window. Scrolling on your phone isn't a break for your brain.

Questions

Things people usually ask.

Is QuietDesk really free?

Yes. There's no paid version, no trial and no advertising. It's a small, simple site that costs very little to run.

Do I need an account?

No. Nothing on this site asks for your name, email or a password.

Does the timer keep going if I switch tabs?

Yes. It checks the real clock rather than counting seconds, so it stays accurate in the background. Some browsers delay sounds in background tabs, so the chime may arrive a moment late. The time shown in the tab title updates while it runs.

Where are my planner and checklist saved?

Only in your own browser, using its local storage. They aren't sent anywhere. If you use a school computer or clear your browser data, they'll be gone, so print anything you want to keep.

Is 25 minutes the right length?

It's a good place to start. Some people focus better in 50-minute blocks, others in 15. Try a few sessions, notice when your attention drifts, and adjust the lengths to match.

Which method should I start with?

If you struggle to get started, try the Pomodoro timer. If you study a lot but forget it, switch from rereading to active recall and space out your reviews. Those two changes make the biggest difference for most students.
